# Nine scores teach Dead Letter when to swell

The nine scores agree on one lesson that Dead Letter has already half-learned: music should be an event, not wallpaper, and each event should be a transformation of very little material. Andrew Prahlow kept Outer Wilds mostly silent so each cue would matter. Marc Streitenfeld left most of *The Grey* to the wind. A *Sheepy* reviewer describes music that recedes and then "swells at key moments". Howard Shore withheld his Fellowship theme until the group was complete, then broke it into shards. These are the same instincts behind Dead Letter's standing direction: sparse music at transitions, and real swells at moments of understanding. Three imports would add the most. The first is Shore's discipline of **withholding**: fragment assembly, plus "decline" versus "ascension" endings on one melody. The second is Prahlow's **distributed diegetic theme**: parts scattered across the world, reassembled at the end, with the last voice joining only by consent. The third is the **costume change** used by Civilization VI and *Sheepy*: one tune, re-orchestrated as eras pass. It maps directly onto Mara's crooked tree, which the story bible already moves from crayon to seedling. One caution covers everything below. The research environment blocked direct page reads, so **every finding about the composers rests on search-engine summaries of the cited pages, not on full reads**. Confidence therefore varies widely. Composer quotes reported by named outlets (Prahlow on "wallpaper", Streitenfeld on writing the orchestra backwards, Shore in Doug Adams' annotated scores, Tin's program notes) are the firmest ground. Phil Lober and Zack Hemsey rest mostly on metadata, forum posts and user reviews. Most structural techniques survive Dead Letter's code-synthesized score. Choirs, folk soloists, brass, and the human "people noises" that carry warmth in Planet Coaster would need real players or sample libraries.

## Every finding here rests on search-result snippets, graded by source

Each composer study was compiled in a session where WebFetch failed on DNS and curl got 403s from the proxy. The researchers could therefore read only the excerpts that search engines returned. Quotes are as those excerpts gave them, and some are partial or paraphrased by the outlet or the summariser. Several of the most valuable primary sources were found but never opened. These include Doug Adams' Annotated Score PDFs for *The Lord of the Rings*, Game Developer's two long Outer Wilds features, the Bandcamp Daily interview with Jim Guthrie, and the Static Mass interview with Streitenfeld. No audio was listened to, and no harmonic claim comes from listening. This report keeps the confidence each note recorded and uses one scale throughout. **Strong** means the composer's own words or an official or publisher page, as relayed by a search extract. **Moderate** means a named critic, trade outlet or Wikipedia. **Weak** means a fan wiki, forum, user review, aggregator or Grokipedia. **Unverified** or **conflicting** marks claims the notes flagged as such. The researchers labelled their own inferences, and those labels are kept here. Everything this report adds about Dead Letter is marked as a **recommendation**, and recommendations borrow technique, never melody.

The game-side facts come from the repository: the standing direction ([direction.md](../docs/direction.md)), the story bible ([story_bible.md](../docs/story_bible.md)), the 51-scene overview ([scene_overview.md](../docs/scene_overview.md)), the prologue audio audit ([audio_audit.md](../docs/audio_audit.md)), the score's own README ([art/music/README.md](../art/music/README.md)) and the `Music`, `Sound` and `Voice` modules in [game/undelivered/index.html](../game/undelivered/index.html). Where these documents disagree, the story bible says it governs.

## Nine studies: what each score does, and how sure we can be

### Tal Richards and Hahlweg, *Sheepy: A Short Adventure*: recede, then swell

The brief needed three corrections. The game was released on **6 February 2024**. Richards **co-composed** it with Hahlweg (Louis Hahlweg), and Yoe Mase features on one track. The solo developer is Thomas Lean, working under MrSuicideSheep ([Steam soundtrack](https://store.steampowered.com/app/2779500/Sheepy_A_Short_Adventure_Soundtrack/); [hahlwegsounds.com](https://www.hahlwegsounds.com/); [Construct blog](https://www.construct.net/en/blogs/construct-official-blog-1/developer-diaries-sheepy-1571)). Richards came from the alt-electronic duo Echos, with credits in the Illenium and Seven Lions orbit ([talrichards.com](https://www.talrichards.com/); [AllMusic](https://www.allmusic.com/artist/tal-richards-mn0003855222)). Reviewers hear the score as electronic with emotional piano, "subtle, melancholic, and beautifully matched to the game's pacing" ([GameCritix](https://gamecritix.co.uk/sheepy-a-short-adventure-review/)).

The best-sourced technique point comes from one review. In it, the music "often fades into the background, allowing ambient sounds to take prominence, but swells at key moments to reinforce emotion", and silence heightens tension and isolation ([GameCritix](https://gamecritix.co.uk/sheepy-a-short-adventure-review/)). That is close to Dead Letter's own direction word for word (moderate, single critic). The 23-track album **re-dresses its own themes**: "The Brine of Before (Music Boxes)", "Enter Over (String Quartet Version)", "Fluorescent North (String Quartet Version)" and "The Violet Pike (Orchestral Version)" ([VGMdb](https://vgmdb.net/album/137492); [Bandcamp](https://sheepy.bandcamp.com/album/sheepy-a-short-adventure-original-soundtrack)). The tracklist is strong evidence, but nothing confirms that these versions play at particular moments in the game. One player singled out the finale, where "you light the 3 towers and your powers were removed one by one" under "great music" ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1568400/discussions/0/4759829899291592013/)). That is weak, but it points to a sharp idea: the score builds while the player is stripped of ability. One outlet calls the score adaptive but gives no mechanism ([CinematicPlatformers](https://cinematicplatformers.com/games/sheepy-a-short-adventure/)). **No harmonic analysis, transcription or composer interview about the score was found**, so the score's harmonic language is unknown.

### Phil Lober, *If*: a debut album, not a soundtrack, with the thinnest evidence of the nine

*If* is not a film or game score. It is Lober's debut solo album, released **31 March 2016**: 11 tracks, about 51 minutes, filed as Electronic and tagged "electronic, epic, cinematic" ([Apple Music](https://music.apple.com/us/album/if/1099348612); [Deezer](https://www.deezer.com/us/album/12777474); [Last.fm](https://www.last.fm/music/Phil+Lober/_/Alive)). Its best-known track, "Alive", is an orchestral song sung by the VOCALOID voicebank AVANNA. It was Lober's first VOCALOID piece ([Vocaloid Wiki](https://vocaloid.fandom.com/wiki/Alive/Phillip_Lober)). The tracklist opens "Almost Alive (Intro)" into "Alive" and moves through "Divide", "Mother", "Toybox" and "Flowers Fall" to "Hokkaido" ([Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/If-Phil-Lober/dp/B01DQDJGLQ)). Automated key detection puts "Alive" in F major at 75 BPM and "Flowers Fall" in B♭ major at 160 BPM ([Tunebat](https://tunebat.com/Info/Alive-Phil-Lober/6ZlLUOdFKtrYoHS2Ih6abW); [Tunebat](https://tunebat.com/Info/Flowers-Fall-Phil-Lober/1YwDHnFoFtctFL1Sy1nXbt)). The notes warn that such detection is often wrong.

The only first-hand craft statements are about trailer work in general. Lober said a trailer packages "an entire emotional plot into a short summary through music and editing" ([Voyage LA](https://voyagela.com/interview/life-work-with-phil-lober)). He also advises studying a successful track's structure without copying it, and to "focus on production" ([Evenant](https://www.evenant.com/articles/5-trailer-music-tips-from-phil-lober)). He reports that listeners first rejected "Alive" for its synthetic voice and later came round. His conclusion was that music "can't fall under labels and be 100% accurate" ([Voyage LA](https://voyagela.com/interview/daily-inspiration-meet-phil-lober/)). **No liner notes, reviews or analysis of *If* exist in the notes.** The two transferable ideas are both researcher inferences from titles and genre tags, so treat them as weak. The first is the intro that "almost" states a theme before the full song. The second is a not-quite-human voice used as an emotional signature.

### Jim Guthrie and JJ Ipsen, Planet Coaster: warmth from human noise, and beds made from the hero material

The album credit is joint and complete: "All music by Jim Guthrie and JJ Ipsen… Performed, recorded and mixed by Jim Guthrie and JJ Ipsen" ([Bandcamp](https://jimguthrie.bandcamp.com/album/you-me-gravity)). It is a home-studio band record, not an orchestral pipeline. A fan wiki says Frontier's audio head wanted a "warm, friendly, honest and authentic audio experience". It also says the first pitch was a thirty-second demo of guitar and whistling ([Planet Coaster Wiki](https://planetcoaster.fandom.com/wiki/Music_of_Planet_Coaster); weak, secondary). The official palette is "acoustic guitars, massive vocals, brass, strings, handclaps, ...even whistlin'" ([Black Screen Records](https://blackscreenrecords.com/products/planet-coaster-original-soundtrack-by-jim-guthrie-jj-ipsen)). Critics heard bright folk-pop with an undertow, "at times introspective and emotional" ([CBC Music](https://www.cbcmusic.ca/first-plays/46/jim-guthrie-jj-ipsen-you-me-gravity)) and "a melancholy journey" ([VGM WAX](https://www.vgmwax.com/reviews/2018/10/17/planet-coaster)). Named tracks include "The Light In Us All", "Theories Of An Eager Heart", "Aspect Imaginarium", the title track and "And So We See The Truth & The Beauty" ([VGMdb](https://vgmdb.net/album/63027)).

Two implementation facts matter most for Dead Letter. First, the ambient album *Weightless* holds tracks that "appear in game as ambient versions of the official soundtrack songs", remixed by Frontier's audio staff ([VGMdb](https://vgmdb.net/album/71069); strong as album metadata). The low-intensity in-game bed was derived from the hero material rather than written separately. Second, Frontier's rule was a soundscape that was "dynamic, adaptive and interactive, and diegetic unless those rules couldn't apply". Much of the park's music plays from placed speakers and rides ([MCV/DEVELOP](https://mcvuk.com/development-news/interview-designing-the-audio-for-planet-coaster/) via the wiki, excerpt-level; [Park Speaker list](https://planetcoaster.fandom.com/wiki/Park_Speaker_-_Music)). Guthrie described aiming for "the most musical version of non-musical ambience", and said a "really fat beat" would be ridiculous in this game ([Bandcamp Daily](https://daily.bandcamp.com/high-scores/jim-guthrie-video-game-soundtrack); excerpt-level). His wider habits include layering near-identical sounds and fading long tape drones in and out ([Native Instruments](https://blog.native-instruments.com/jim-guthrie-from-guitar-music-to-scoring-games/); excerpt-level). Not all the in-game music is theirs: other composers wrote parts of the park library ([forum thread](https://forums.planetcoaster.com/showthread.php/12356-Jim-Guthrie-JJ-Ipsen-present-You-Me-Gravity-The-Music-of-Planet-Coaster)). **No harmonic analysis and no Ipsen interview exist in the notes.**

### Andrew Prahlow, Outer Wilds: silence as policy, one song split across a solar system

The firmest statement in the whole research comes from Prahlow. "When there's constant looping music in an exploration game like Outer Wilds, the music can lose importance and become wallpaper… I crafted the music to follow the player's sense of exploration" ([Game Developer 2019](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/behind-the-hauntingly-beautiful-music-of-i-outer-wilds-i-); strong). He treated the game "like a film", with music only in emotionally important segments ([Prima Games](https://primagames.com/featured/developing-music-outer-wilds-interview-composer-andrew-prahlow)). "There's no music playing for most of the game, so when the music enters it's really important" ([Film Music Central](https://filmmusiccentral.com/2020/03/26/an-interview-with-andrew-prahlow-composer-of-outer-wilds/)). His workflow was to write suites first, then cut them into loops with fades ([Film Music Central](https://filmmusiccentral.com/2020/03/26/an-interview-with-andrew-prahlow-composer-of-outer-wilds/)). The home palette came from a cheap source: "an old beat up banjo that I had received as a gift", chosen for "simplicity and nostalgia" and set against ambient, post-rock-influenced textures for the alien Nomai ([Film Music Central](https://filmmusiccentral.com/2020/03/26/an-interview-with-andrew-prahlow-composer-of-outer-wilds/); [USC Thornton](https://music.usc.edu/andrew-prahlow-earns-two-award-nominations-for-outer-wilds-soundtrack/)).

The signature device is the "Travelers" song. Each traveller plays one part of a single song on a different instrument, each on a different planet ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_Wilds)). The player can hear those parts from anywhere with the Signalscope. At the Eye they assemble around a campfire into the full piece ([Outer Wilds Wiki OST](https://outerwilds.fandom.com/wiki/Outer_Wilds_OST); [SVG](https://www.svg.com/385123/outer-wilds-ending-explained/)). Prahlow says the one-song design was planned with director Alex Beachum from the start ([SPIN](https://www.spinmagazine.com/2020/06/outer-wilds-andrew-prahlow/); the exact wording is unconfirmed). He chose instruments from Beachum's character sketches ([Game Developer 2022](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/everything-but-lost-exploring-the-mesmerising-musical-legacy-of-outer-wilds)). In Echoes of the Eye, the estranged Prisoner "ask[s] the Protagonist if they are allowed to join" before adding a last voice ([Outer Wilds Wiki](https://outerwilds.fandom.com/wiki/Prisoner); weak, fan wiki). That voice is a Moog blended with "a haunted slide guitar", not a theremin ([Game Developer 2022](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/everything-but-lost-exploring-the-mesmerising-musical-legacy-of-outer-wilds)). Two functional cues stand out. "End Times" plays in the last two minutes of the loop, is audible anywhere, and ends as the sun goes supernova ([Outer Wilds Wiki](https://outerwilds.fandom.com/wiki/Supernova)). TV Tropes says it starts about 90 seconds out, so the timing **conflicts** ([TV Tropes](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/VideoGame/OuterWilds)). "Castaways" plays at two separate tragedy sites, the Interloper's core and the Dark Bramble grave ([Outer Wilds Wiki OST](https://outerwilds.fandom.com/wiki/Outer_Wilds_OST)), so one cue binds two discoveries. **No developer source documents how the stems are synchronised**, so the implementation is inferred. The score was BAFTA-nominated in 2020 ([USC Thornton](https://music.usc.edu/andrew-prahlow-nominated-for-bafta-games-award/)), and Echoes won the 2022 G.A.N.G. indie music award ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Prahlow)).

### Christopher Tin and Geoff Knorr, Civilization: one melody, four costumes

Tin's two menu anthems share a plan. A sacred or aspirational text in a language other than English sits inside the vocal tradition that language implies, and the music then climbs. His program note says "Baba Yetu" (Civ IV, the Lord's Prayer in Swahili) adds "an explosive orchestral accompaniment and a complex series of modulations" to a Swahili choral tradition of call-and-response ([christophertin.com](https://christophertin.com/blogs/works/baba-yetu); strong). Sources **disagree** on its keys. Tin wanted Civ VI's "Sogno di Volare" "stately and grand, like a national anthem". Its chorus adapts the Leonardo flight quote from Civ IV's tech tree, which ties the two games together ([christophertin.com](https://christophertin.com/blogs/recordings/sogno-di-volare-civilization-vi)). A pipe organ arrives only in its final bars ([nerdpool.it](https://nerdpool.it/civilization-vi-compie-dieci-anni-un-gioco-imperfetto-che-ha-vinto-restando-in-piedi); moderate-to-weak, secondary). "Baba Yetu" won a Grammy in February 2011, reported as the first for video game music ([Grammy.com](https://grammy.com/news/and-the-grammy-went-to-christopher-tin)). Its category name differs between sources.

Knorr's Civ VI system plays each civilization's theme in four era arrangements. They run from "simple single instrument melodies in the Ancient Era" to modern settings by the Atomic era ([Civilization Soundtrack wiki](https://civilization-soundtrack.fandom.com/wiki/Civilization_VI); weak). Between those, a small ensemble with strings and light percussion gives way to full orchestra with brass ([LadiesGamers](https://ladiesgamers.com/paulas-game-ramblings-music-of-civilization-vi/)). The arrangements "gradually build to grand, intricate, sweeping orchestral-electronic arrangements over the course of hundreds of turns" ([Knorr GANG FYC](https://www.geoffknorr.com/gang-fyc); attribution ambiguous). The credits show culture-bearer performers recording the Ancient "seed" versions and the house team arranging the later eras ([Bandcamp](https://sidmeierscivilization.bandcamp.com/album/sid-meiers-civilization-vi-original-game-soundtrack)). Civ V instead paired **Peace and War** playlists for four regions. Its leader themes were built on real, recognisable melodies, "often only hinting at the theme" ([VGMO](https://vgmonline.net/curranknorrinterview/)). One war variation is "agitated by stabbing strings" yet keeps "a longing for peace in its interludes" ([Square Enix Music Online](https://www.squareenixmusic.com/reviews/chris/civilization5.shtml)). **How the era switch or the peace/war switch works in code is undocumented**, and the notes treat any crossfade or stem claim as unverified.

### Zack Hemsey: one ostinato, many layers, and a hit that went stale

Hemsey is a self-taught, in-the-box composer. "The only gear he uses regularly is his Genelec monitors and Logic" ([The Audio Spotlight](https://theaudiospotlight.com/zack-hemsey-interview/)). He is known for "Mind Heist", the music for Inception's third trailer, which is not part of Zimmer's film score ([Inception wiki](https://inception.fandom.com/wiki/Mind_Heist); [Collider](https://collider.com/inception-trailer-musical-cue/)), and for "Vengeance", used in *The Equalizer* and *The Last Dance* ([Tunefind](https://www.tunefind.com/song/zack-hemsey/vengeance)). The BRAAAM lineage is moderately sourced. Mike Zarin's teaser treated the low horn blast as sound design, and Zarin says Hemsey gave his version "a musical quality" ([Collider](https://collider.com/inception-trailer-musical-cue/); [Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/movies/movie-news/braaams-beginners-how-a-horn-793220/)). Zimmer has said he dislikes the overuse of the Inception BRAAAM in trailers ([Dark Horizons](https://www.darkhorizons.com/zimmer-hates-inception-braaam-in-trailers/)).

The technique evidence is mostly weak. One forum reaction says "Mind Heist" uses samples "in a very rhythmical/upfront unrealistic way" and that it "build[s] up very ingeniously" with risers ([VI-Control](https://vi-control.net/community/threads/mind-heist-zack-hemsey.32920/)). Hemsey's own blog explains that trailer cuts are abridged from longer works ([Hemsey blog](http://zackhemsey.blogspot.com/2013/11/braaam-101.html)). "Mind Heist (Evolution)" runs to about six minutes and builds "towards a massive sounding climax" ([filmdetail](https://filmdetail.com/2011/05/16/new-versions-of-mind-heist-zack-hemsey-inception/)). His *Goliath* is a "soundtrack to a film that doesn't exist" ([Discogs](https://www.discogs.com/release/11717101-Zack-Hemsey-Goliath-Original-Motion-Picture-Soundtrack-To-A-Film-That-Doesnt-Exist)). He says writing it was "no different than any other album" ([Viral Hare](https://viralhare.com/a-conversation-with-composer-zack-hemsey-featured-in-inception-equalizer/)). User reviews of *Ronin* find many tracks repetitive at around six minutes ([RYM](https://rateyourmusic.com/release/album/zack-hemsey/ronin/)). Key estimates for "Mind Heist" **conflict**: A♭ major at 92 BPM against C minor at 188 ([Tunebat](https://tunebat.com/Info/Mind-Heist-Zack-Hemsey/3P5OnuqwVhZMEMoaZa9GuE); [Chordify](https://chordify.net/chords/zack-hemsey-songs/mind-heist-chords)). The albums "Pillars" and "Alpha" named in the brief **could not be confirmed to exist**. Of the nine, Hemsey's emotional register is the furthest from Dead Letter's timid, whimsical tone. His value here is structural: long arcs built over one repeating figure, and an unashamedly synthetic sound.

### Marc Streitenfeld: making every sound, writing the orchestra backwards, and leaving the wilderness unscored

Streitenfeld is largely self-taught and started as an assistant to Hans Zimmer. He then worked as a music editor and supervisor on Ridley Scott films before Scott made him a composer ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marc_Streitenfeld)). He told Variety: "I make all the sounds you hear in my scores", with no sample libraries ([Variety](https://variety.com/2015/music/features/composer-marc-streitenfeld-is-arduous-in-his-search-for-the-lost-chord-1201501614/); strong). For *Prometheus*: "I actually wrote out the sheet music backwards so the orchestra played it backwards and then I digitally flipped it… the same melody, but with a backwards sounding orchestra which gives it a kind of unusual, unsettling sound" ([Static Mass](http://staticmass.net/exclusive-interviews/marc-streitenfeld-prometheus-composer/); strong, via extract). He phrased the recording so the flipped version would have "a different expression and dynamic". He started the treatment with woodwinds and flutes ([Static Mass](http://staticmass.net/exclusive-interviews/marc-streitenfeld-prometheus-composer/)). The noble "Life" theme that recurs at key moments was Harry Gregson-Williams', and reviewers say it displaced much of Streitenfeld's own thematic material ([Movie Music UK](https://moviemusicuk.us/2012/06/20/prometheus-marc-streitenfeld/)). Filmtracks found the score's four themes incoherent and its sound design intrusive ([Filmtracks](https://www.filmtracks.com/titles/prometheus.html)).

*The Grey* is the restraint case. The director wanted a minimal underscore, and strings perform the only themes "alongside tastefully muted keyboarding" ([Filmtracks](https://www.filmtracks.com/titles/grey.html)). Much of the film was "left unscored", relying mainly on the Alaskan wind and other sound effects ([Tracksounds](http://blog.tracksounds.com/2012/02/grey-soundtrack-by-marc-streitenfeld.html)). *Robin Hood* uses lute, fiddles, uilleann pipes and hurdy-gurdy with no synths ([Filmtracks](https://www.filmtracks.com/titles/robin_hood10.html)), over "driving rock rhythms" that are "pure 21st century" ([OUP Blog](https://blog.oup.com/2010/05/robin-hood-score/)). *Poltergeist* is described in terms of "creaky sound patterns" and a "twisted timbre" ([Sony Classical](https://sony.mediaroom.com/2015-05-07-Sony-Classical-Releases-Poltergeist-Soundtrack)). Across his work, critics repeat that the scores serve the film but disappoint on album, as with *The Grey* on Movie-Wave ([Movie-Wave](https://www.movie-wave.net/the-grey/)). That makes his catalogue a warning as much as a model: texture-first scoring needs at least one protected theme. The notes also correct a premise of the brief: *Welcome to Marwen* is Alan Silvestri's score, not Streitenfeld's ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welcome_to_Marwen_(soundtrack))).

### Hans Zimmer, *The Last Samurai* and *Interstellar*: a seed written away from the plot, and silence before the peak

Nolan briefed Zimmer with a one-page fable about a parent leaving a child, written before Zimmer knew the film's genre. Zimmer answered overnight with a roughly four-minute piece for piano and organ, "Day One" ([Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/news/general-news/composer-hans-zimmer-talks-interstellar-745891/); [Classic FM](https://www.classicfm.com/composers/zimmer/how-wrote-interstellar-music/)). The "I'll come back / When?" content of the letter reaches us only through non-English secondary reports, so it is **unconfirmed**. The organ was Nolan's suggestion. Zimmer heard it as both mournful and spiritual ([Den of Geek](https://www.denofgeek.com/us/movies/hans-zimmer/241533/hans-zimmer-talks-about-scoring-interstellar-and-why-batman-needs-a-new-theme)). He drafted on a sampled Salisbury Cathedral organ before recording the 1926 Harrison & Harrison at Temple Church ([Hauptwerk](https://www.hauptwerk.com/hauptwerk-goes-interstellar/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstellar_(soundtrack))). Nolan kept the organ's lowest sub-frequencies in a "very, very radical mix", and critics complained the bass buried dialogue ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/nolan-sound-complaints)). Analysts describe the main theme as a VI–VII–i loop in A minor over a held E pedal ([Point Blank](https://plus.pointblankmusicschool.com/analysing-hans-zimmers-interstellar-theme-song/); voicings disputed). In "No Time for Caution", an organ figure drops into half time while a ticking moves into double time ([Envato](https://elements.envato.com/learn/interstellar-soundtrack)).

Three *Interstellar* points bear directly on timing, but their sources are weak or student-level. The tick in "Mountains" is said to fall every 1.25 seconds, one Earth day per tick, but that is a Reddit user's calculation ([BuzzFeed](https://www.buzzfeed.com/christopherhudspeth/this-interstellar-easter-egg-is-shocking)). The tick reportedly disappears under the crescendo and returns faster ([UBC blog](https://blogs.ubc.ca/musc326a/?p=735)). The strongest silence, one to three seconds after Dr. Mann's failed docking, precedes the score's biggest cue ([Berklee paper](https://remix.berklee.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?filename=2&article=1073&context=graduate-studies-scoring&type=additional)). The wormhole sequences dropped the score entirely because it "competed too much with the sound effects" ([CineMontage](https://cinemontage.org/?p=2878); moderate). Filmtracks dissents from the praise. It says Zimmer "accesses the same set of rhythmic structures and applies volume to them in identical ways in most cues" ([Filmtracks](https://www.filmtracks.com/titles/interstellar.html)).

*The Last Samurai* plants its peaceful "way of life" theme on koto and shakuhachi in "A Way of Life". "Idyll's End" turns it into a brass statement in its last two minutes, as the village's peace ends ([Movie Music UK](https://moviemusicuk.us/2003/12/05/the-last-samurai-hans-zimmer/)). The close, "A Small Measure of Peace", rises "from the silence" ([Soundtrackworld](https://soundtrackworld.com/2017/08/the-last-samurai-hans-zimmer/)). Filmtracks puts the score's power in its "cues of lesser intensity" ([Filmtracks](https://www.filmtracks.com/titles/last_samurai.html)). Zimmer's stated approach was Western themes with a Japanese aesthetic ([hans-zimmer.com](https://hans-zimmer.com/product/the-last-samurai/); paraphrase, date uncertain). The often-repeated claim of 10,000 processed taiko hits is **unverified**.

### Howard Shore, *The Lord of the Rings*: families, withholding and cadence substitution

Shore wrote "over 50 leitmotifs" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_The_Lord_of_the_Rings_film_series)). In his words they were "used for clarity in storytelling" ([Ludwig-Van](https://www.ludwig-van.com/toronto/2021/12/17/feature-composer-howard-shore-looks-back-on-his-work-for-the-lord-of-the-rings/); strong). Cultures are marked before any melody starts. Isengard is in 5/4 with taiko, anvils and chains ([LotR fandom](https://lotr.fandom.com/wiki/Soundtracks_of_The_Lord_of_the_Rings_film_trilogy); weak). The Dwarves have parallel fifths for male choir, Rohan has the Hardanger fiddle ([TheOneRing.net](https://theonering.net/torwp/2003/01/08/22545-the-rohan-theme-and-the-fiddle)), and Gollum's Menace has the cimbalom ([Movie Music UK](https://moviemusicuk.us/2002/12/20/the-lord-of-the-rings-the-two-towers-howard-shore/)). Doug Adams writes that the augmented second in the Lothlórien mode signals "a sense of age", not otherness ([lotsoflux, quoting Adams](https://lotsoflux.wordpress.com/2010/10/05/lotr-prologue-in-hijaz/)). The Ring has three themes. The History of the Ring sounds when the Ring changes hands, in Shore's words: "He's physically touched it… so it's passed from Bilbo to Frodo" ([FotR Annotated Score](https://www.elvish.org/gwaith/pdf/fotr_annotated_score_2.pdf); strong via extract). The Seduction of the Ring uses boys' voices for "false purity" ([Macksey Journal](https://mackseyjournal.scholasticahq.com/api/v1/articles/28026-even-darkness-must-pass-an-ethical-commentary-and-musical-analysis-of-_the-lord-of-the-rings_-score.pdf)). The same boy-soprano timbre carries the genuine renewal of Nature's Reclamation ([amagpiesnest](https://www.amagpiesnest.com/themes/THnature.htm)).

The transformations are the core lesson. The Fellowship theme "is assembled by fragments throughout the first film and scattered into shards thereafter" ([LotR in Concert](https://www.lordoftheringsinconcert.com/fotr)). Shore, before Rivendell: "it's not completely assembled, but it's getting closer because now Strider has joined them" ([FotR Annotated Score](https://www.elvish.org/gwaith/pdf/fotr_annotated_score_2.pdf)). Gondor's theme is repeatedly left on its unresolved "Decline" figure ([RotK Annotated Score](https://www.tolkiendil.com/_media/divertissements/adaptations/films/sda_3_livret_de_presentation_de_la_bo_en_anglais_.pdf)). Even the Lighting of the Beacons, a "magniloquent statement", still ends on it ([Last.fm wiki](https://www.last.fm/music/Howard+Shore/_/The+Lighting+of+the+Beacons/+wiki)). Only when Aragorn's theme attaches does an ascending ending replace the Decline figure ([LotR fandom](https://lotr.fandom.com/wiki/Music_of_the_Middle-earth_film_series)). The Pity of Gollum theme appears "very little" in *The Return of the King*, where his Menace music dominates ([amagpiesnest](https://www.amagpiesnest.com/themes/THgollum_pity.htm)). In "The Bridge of Khazad-dûm" the choir enters when Gandalf names the Balrog ([Film Music Central](https://filmmusiccentral.com/2016/08/11/the-fellowship-of-the-ring-the-bridge-of-khazad-dum-2001/)). The instrumental material of "Into the West" is planted in "The Eagles" and "The Journey to the Grey Havens" before the song itself plays over the credits ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Into_the_West_(song))). The awards are secure: Best Original Score Oscars for *Fellowship* and *Return of the King*, plus Best Original Song for "Into the West" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings:_The_Return_of_the_King_(soundtrack))). Bar-level analysis of the coronation or the Beacons was **not reached**.

## Seven techniques recur across the nine scores

The first technique has the strongest consensus in the research: **music as an event against a ground of ambience**. Prahlow says it outright ("wallpaper"). *The Grey* leaves its wilderness to wind and effects. *Interstellar* drops the score from the wormhole because it fought the sound design. *Sheepy* "fades into the background" and swells at key moments. Guthrie's phrase "the most musical version of non-musical ambience" describes the same boundary from the other side. When five unrelated sources independently converge like this, the evidence is good even though each source is a snippet. Dead Letter's direction already asks for exactly this ([direction.md](../docs/direction.md)).

The second technique is **one theme in many costumes**. Recognition rests on the melody and meaning comes from the orchestration. Civ VI walks a single civilization's tune from solo folk instrument to orchestra and electronics. *Sheepy* reissues its tunes for music boxes and string quartet. Planet Coaster's in-game beds are ambient versions of its songs. *The Last Samurai* moves its pastoral theme from shakuhachi to brass at "Idyll's End". The common lesson is economy: few melodies, many settings, each setting chosen for a story state.

The third technique is **withholding and assembly**. The real statement of a theme is kept back so that its arrival is recognised, not merely heard. Shore assembles the Fellowship from fragments and leaves Gondor on its Decline figure until Aragorn claims his place. Prahlow scatters one song across a solar system and assembles it at a campfire. Tin holds the pipe organ back until the last bars of "Sogno di Volare". Lober's "Almost Alive (Intro)" before "Alive" may be a modest version of the same idea, but that rests only on titles. Shore's version is the most useful because it works through form rather than silence: the theme is present, but incomplete or wrongly ended.

The fourth technique is **a signature timbre with a cultural meaning, used against expectation**. Examples are a gift banjo in space, a church organ in a science-fiction film, a Hardanger fiddle for Rohan, a reversed orchestra for alien makers, and a synthetic voice over an orchestra. In each case one sound carries more identity than a melody could. The boy soprano shows that one timbre can carry opposite meanings: false purity for the Ring, real renewal for nature.

The fifth technique is **time made audible**. "End Times" teaches Outer Wilds players to hear the end of the loop without a clock. The "Mountains" tick makes time dilation felt, and "No Time for Caution" stacks a half-time figure against a double-time tick. The *Sheepy* finale reportedly builds music while removing the player's powers one by one. These tie a musical pulse to something the story or the player cares about.

The sixth technique is **diegetic sources as score**. Outer Wilds' travellers play in the world, and Planet Coaster's speakers and rides play in the park. *American Gangster* blurs score into period source music ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Gangster_(soundtrack))). The warrior shouts in "Red Warrior" are used melodically ([Movie Music UK](https://moviemusicuk.us/2003/12/05/the-last-samurai-hans-zimmer/)). In each case the world's own sound sources become part of the score.

The seventh technique is **identity by family rather than tune**. Shore's cultures are identified by mode, timbre and metre before any theme plays. Prahlow splits his palette into folk for the living and synth for the dead. Civ V divides its music by region and by peace or war. *The Grey* runs two unrelated registers, one for grief and one for the predator. Running under all seven is a counter-lesson from the critics. Filmtracks faults *Interstellar* for applying volume "in identical ways" and Streitenfeld for scores that vanish without picture. Zimmer himself tires of his BRAAAM. Additive swells and texture banks wear out unless they are rationed and anchored to a protected theme.

|---|---|---|---|

## Dead Letter already has the skeleton: one lullaby, eleven cues, a parcel's voice

Dead Letter's score is a written score rendered by code. `compose_dl01.py` builds every cue from small synthesized instruments, so "the music can be rebuilt and changed like any other asset" ([art/music/README.md](../art/music/README.md)). Those instruments are a felt piano, a slightly out-of-tune music box, additive bowed strings (violin, viola, cello, bass), solo lines with portamento, soft-mallet timpani rolls, a suspended-cymbal swell, a far bell, wind, a convolution hall and tape wow, flutter and hiss. The theme is a **D-minor lullaby in 3/4 at 60 bpm** in two eight-bar phrases. Phrase A turns on a falling F–E–D figure. Phrase B climbs and opens onto E♭maj7, "the widest moment", before falling back to A. Those first three notes, F–E–D, played on the music box, are "the parcel's voice" ([art/music/README.md](../art/music/README.md)). The game plays the eleven rendered cues through a `Music` module. It plays one cue at a time with a fade-in (1.2 s by default), loops a 48-second bed between cues, queues one cue after another, and ducks to 40 per cent under any voice ([index.html](../game/undelivered/index.html)).

The scene numbering in the music README predates the current eleven-scene prologue. Matched against the code's triggers and the scene documents, the cues now fall as follows. `wake` plays under the opening of DL-01, `hall` at the first entry to DL-01's sorting hall, and `threshold` as DL-01's bulkhead opens into DL-02. `racks` is the cold lament as the couriers are revealed at DL-03's gate. `kin` is the realisation among the couriers at DL-04: phrase A broadens over about 25 seconds through cello, violins in octaves, piano, a timpani roll and a cymbal swell into bar 8, then lets go to the music box. `dead` covers DL-06's refusal, the pit and the label check, and turns for the first time to F major. `latch` is the pawl at DL-08. `refuge` starts at DL-09's balcony over the chasm city, after the tunnel, and plays out into DL-10. `child` plays as the desk clears the growth marks at DL-11 and is reused at DL-14's peeled panel and DL-15's dismissal. `end` plays over the end card, where the music box plays *Happy Birthday* and "stops one phrase short" ([art/music/README.md](../art/music/README.md); [index.html](../game/undelivered/index.html); [DL-06](../docs/scenes/DL-06_return_to_sender.md)). That is already a faithful implementation of the direction's swell: violins in octaves, a timpani roll, a suspended-cymbal swell, release, then room for the music box ([direction.md](../docs/direction.md)).

Around the score sits a sound world that is mostly unused musically. The audio audit adds positional **emitters**: drip, clock tick, fluorescent buzz, water, wind, fan, far machine, radio static and a **music box**. It also adds the **voice-card player** (a wooden box with a brass horn and a crank) and **tannoys** that announce as D-17 passes. All of these run through an **old-recording chain**: speed and pitch, wow and flutter, saturation, band-pass, crackle and room ([audio_audit.md](../docs/audio_audit.md)). Two details matter for what follows. The den's music-box emitter at DL-09 plays its own short tune from C (scale steps 0, 4, 7, 9, 7, 4, 2, 4, 0, −3, 0). That is **not** the parcel's F–E–D motif, and it is synthesized in the browser rather than by the score's music-box instrument ([index.html](../game/undelivered/index.html)). The radio at DL-11 finds "a few bars of a children's song" in static before `radio_above` brings Mara's old voice, "the first living voice in the game" ([index.html](../game/undelivered/index.html); [voice_script.md](../docs/voice_script.md)). The story gives the score its spine. Mara is the last birth, R.Y. 34. The parcel is her ninth-birthday present ("It's for outside"). The motto ORDER ABOVE ALL is a refrain that will be painted over, defaced and finally scratched out. Mara's crooked tree runs from crayon to a real seedling. The all-hope-is-lost moment is the birth register's last line, near minute 127 ([story_bible.md](../docs/story_bible.md); [voice_script.md](../docs/voice_script.md)).

## Recommendations: the techniques mapped onto Dead Letter's scenes

Everything in this section is the report's own musical reasoning, built on the techniques above and on the repository. None of it is a finding about the composers. It borrows procedures (withholding, assembly, re-orchestration, silence), never a melody, rhythm or figure from any of the scores studied.

### Build a small theme family, not more themes

**Recommendation.** Keep the lullaby as the only real melody, and add three non-melodic or near-melodic family members, following Shore's rule that a family is mode, timbre and rhythm before it is a tune. The first is the **parcel motif**: the music box's F–E–D, used as Shore uses the History of the Ring. It sounds when the parcel changes hands or is presented: at DL-11's terminal, when Mara touches the wrapper at DL-43, and at DL-51. The second is an **Order signature**, built from the tannoy's two-note chime (the `tannoy` treatment already plays a chime) and a strict duple or odd-metre machine pulse that never agrees with the lullaby's 3/4. This borrows Shore's use of metre as a moral marker, as with Isengard's 5/4, and gives the refuge's institutions a fingerprint without a theme. The third is **Mara's tree**, drawn from phrase B's climb to the E♭ "widest moment", which is already the most aspiring gesture in the theme. Shore's contour borrowing is the model for keeping these related: the tree figure should share an interval cell with the parcel motif, so the parent's gift and the child's tree audibly belong together. The voice script makes that link explicit: "Keep drawing your trees" ([voice_script.md](../docs/voice_script.md)).

**Recommendation.** Treat `card_mara_parent` as the score's "Day One" brief. Zimmer's seed worked because it was written from a one-page human story rather than the setting. Dead Letter already has that page: the birthday card's forty seconds about a parcel "for outside" and trees that need not be straight. Every new cue can be tested against it.

### Walk Mara's tree through the eras, Civilization VI style

**Recommendation.** The story bible moves the tree through crayon (prologue), pencil (Attendance), graffiti (Someone Home), engineering drawings (The Work Above) and a real seedling at the end. Score that journey as Knorr scores a civilization: one tune, re-orchestrated era by era. At DL-11 and DL-14 the tree figure belongs to the music box alone. At DL-17, where the crooked tree "becomes a rough irrigation drawing beside a repaired pot" ([scene_overview.md](../docs/scene_overview.md)), it moves to the felt piano with one string. Through the Commons it becomes a **string quartet**, a few players for a community of few, re-dressed as *Sheepy* re-dressed its tunes. At the midpoint, DL-30 The First Leaf, it takes its fullest orchestral form so far. The overview asks that "water and leaf movement carry the release before any music enters", which fits Zimmer's silence-then-build. The seedling at the end returns it, small, to the music box: Shore's "smallest theme at the highest moment".

### Withhold the resolution the way Shore withholds Gondor

**Recommendation.** Give the theme two endings and ration them. The "decline" ending is the open A that `hall` and `racks` already stay on. The "ascension" ending is a full cadence to D major, like the Picardy close in `end`. Use the open-A ending everywhere the story is still unresolved, including grand moments such as the `refuge` reveal, as Shore ends even the Beacons on Decline. Reserve the full D-major cadence for DL-51. The direction's "theme returning in a major key" after each low point is already satisfied by the turn to F major (the relative major) in `dead` and `latch` ([direction.md](../docs/direction.md)). The tonic major can then stay unspent. There is a trade-off. The prologue build's end card currently resolves to D major, and a standalone demo needs a satisfying close. Once more chapters exist, though, the end card should stop resolving.

**Recommendation.** Let *Happy Birthday*, which runs down "one phrase short" over the end card, finish only at DL-51, when Mara opens the boots. This is Shore's Grey Havens plant (material heard long before its full arrival) combined with his assembly principle. The README notes the tune is in the US public domain ([art/music/README.md](../art/music/README.md)).

### Scatter the song through the refuge and assemble it at DL-51

**Recommendation.** Use the emitters and voice cards as Prahlow uses the Travelers. Several diegetic sources already carry tunes: the den's music box at DL-09, the radio's children's song at DL-11, the lesson loop at DL-13 (which "sticks on a word when it is braked"), and the tannoy chime. Make each one carry **one voice of a single short piece**, written so each part works alone and in combination. In later chapters, add a hummed kitchen rota in the Commons and a work-crew whistle in The Work Above. The den's tune is currently unrelated to the parcel motif. That can become deliberate: let it be the **children's** tune, which later grows into the teenagers' Den (`teen_den_2`). At DL-51, once the boots are delivered, the scattered parts sound together for the first time, quietly, with wind continuing as the overview asks.

**Recommendation.** Borrow the Prisoner's consent beat for the voluntary sit. The overview ends with D-17 free to sit beside Mara, and insists on "no hidden punishment for waiting" ([scene_overview.md](../docs/scene_overview.md)). So the assembled piece should play whether or not he sits, and **one last voice should join only when the player chooses to sit**: D-17's own, perhaps the music box's parcel motif or his chirp tuned into the chord. Joining is then an act of company, as the Prisoner's was, rather than a reward for compliance.

### Make the protocols' tenderness audibly counterfeit

**Recommendation.** Shore gave the Ring's "false purity" the same boy-soprano timbre that later carries nature's real renewal. Dead Letter's equivalent is the protection protocols: care that smothered. In Attendance and Protective Custody, let the institutional machinery speak in a music-box-like chime. Give it a more regular, mechanical tuning than the parcel's wobbling box, whose tines are deliberately off by up to ±5 cents. The player then learns to hear the difference between a parent's tenderness and the protocol's. DL-12's register voice ("Thank you. You are present.") and the attendant's padded cradle in DL-34 are natural places to plant it.

### Score the Commons with people, and let the empty chair lose a voice

**Recommendation.** Planet Coaster's warmth came from "people noises": whistling, claps and group voices. The Commons is the story's softening, with shared kitchens and rotas signed by everyone. That is the one stretch where the palette should sound performed and plural, and it is the stretch least served by synthesis (see the next section). Within the existing toolset, the strongest move is structural. At DL-21 The Evening Cycle, set phrase A as a two-voice duet, for example cello with viola, in the "matching motions for two bowls". At DL-23 The Second Place, where the same rhythm now serves "a vacant place", play the same duet with one voice missing. That is Shore's "shards" applied to a marriage. At DL-20 the overview asks for no score cue, and at DL-25 "the reduction in domestic noise" is the music. Both are Streitenfeld's *Grey* principle: let the room carry it.

**Recommendation.** When the duet returns at DL-44, as Mara and D-17 complete one repair with two pairs of hands, give the second voice to the **music box**, not the missing viola. The overview says their cooperation must not pretend "to replace the partner she lost". A new timbre in the old second part says exactly that.

### Use the protocols' machinery as time, and silence at the register

**Recommendation.** Protective Custody is where time-made-audible belongs. The attendant's retrieval cycle (DL-35, DL-37, DL-39) can drive a tick or pulse layer whose rate tracks the machine's trip. Under a large movement it can be masked and then return faster, as the "Mountains" tick reportedly does. The fixed retrieval rule makes this fair, because the pulse carries information the player can use. At DL-34's first reach, a single low, swelling blast can sell the alarm before the padded cradle reveals the machine's care. Hemsey's history argues for using it **once** and resolving it into a warm chord at the deposit, never as a recurring stinger.

**Recommendation.** For the breach and the register, near minute 127, follow Zimmer's silence before the peak and Prahlow's restraint. The `ward_intercom` recording is "someone counting the people in the ward out loud, the count going down, a seal closing" ([story_bible.md](../docs/story_bible.md)). The counting is already a tick, so the score should stay out of it entirely. When the seal closes, cut music and most effects for one to three seconds. Then, at the register's last line, let the parcel motif sound alone on the music box, unresolved. Only after `mara_night`'s "I'm going to fix something" should the direction's turn come: the theme in a major key, still ending on the open A.

**Recommendation.** At DL-40, D-17's knee buckles after a solved transfer. Borrow the *Sheepy* finale's subtraction: thin the texture to a single line at the buckle, then let a low sustained string chord enter as the cradle holds him. That way the support is heard as well as seen.

### Bind the dead with one lament, the way "Castaways" binds two graves

**Recommendation.** The direction asks for set pieces of the refuge's failure: sorters dead at their posts, the famine's rows, the crashed goods lift, the cut bridge, the crush at the last car, the drowned street ([direction.md](../docs/direction.md)). Prahlow reused "Castaways" at two tragedy sites so the player would feel the link before understanding it. A short fragment of `racks` can do the same for the refuge's dead: its low D pedal and the unresolved D against E♭ on the piano, a few seconds, never the full cue. The `child` cue already does this for Mara's traces at DL-11, DL-14 and DL-15. Keep the two bindings separate: `racks` for the dead, `child` for Mara.

### Meet Mara with a timbre never heard before, and open the tape at the threshold

**Recommendation.** At the Bridge of Khazad-dûm, Shore's choir enters on the naming of the Balrog. A new timbre's first entrance is strongest when it coincides with an identification. DL-42, Mara's outstretched hand, is the game's identification beat: the imagined recipient becomes a person. Give her presence a sound the score has never used. The most practical is a **whistled or hummed** phrase, the human instrument behind Guthrie's pitch demo and Esker's part in Outer Wilds. The director already records the voice lines, so the pipeline exists.

**Recommendation.** Every cue now passes through tape wow and hiss, and every human voice passes through a band-pass between roughly 250 and 3,500 Hz. At DL-49 The Threshold, with "ordinary daylight, moving leaves and distant water" and no triumphant panorama, remove the tape and the band-limit. The first full-bandwidth, steady-pitched sound in the game should be outside. This is Tin's organ-in-the-last-bars principle, a timbre saved for the end. Here the saved colour is clarity itself, and it costs nothing in code.

### Keep Hemsey's and Zimmer's scale on a short leash

**Recommendation.** Dead Letter's tone is "timid, curious and whimsical", and its one wide shot comes "about once a chapter" ([direction.md](../docs/direction.md)). The additive ostinato build that runs through Hemsey and *Interstellar* should therefore be rationed to the few mechanical climaxes with real scale: the DL-09 cage, DL-29's root lift, and DL-40. It should be written as Hemsey's long arc with exportable intensity tiers, not as a new swell each time. Filmtracks' complaint about identical volume curves is the test. Each swell in `kin`, `dead` and `refuge` already peaks differently, and future ones should keep doing so.

## What code synthesis can carry, and what needs players

Most of the structural lessons survive Dead Letter's code-rendered approach because they are decisions about form, not sound. Withholding, cadence substitution, re-orchestration among the existing instruments, distributed diegetic parts, silence and pulse layers are all composition and mixing. Streitenfeld's backwards orchestra suits the pipeline especially well: rendering a line forward and reversing the array is trivial in numpy. His advice to phrase *for* the reversal applies just as well to synthetic envelopes. Some techniques need runtime work rather than new sounds. The `Music` module plays one cue at a time, so Fellowship-style assembly and Travelers-style stems need a small layering facility: several synchronised buffers with independent gains. The emitters would need a shared clock to keep diegetic parts in time. The sub-bass organ pedal is easy to synthesize, but laptop speakers will not reproduce it, and Nolan's mix shows the cost to dialogue. Any felt low end should be a headphone bonus, never a carrier of meaning (this is a recommendation, consistent with the scene overview's rule that important sound needs a visual equivalent).

| Technique | Source composers | In code now | Survives synthesis? | Needs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sparse cues, silence, ducking | Prahlow, Streitenfeld, *Sheepy*, Zimmer | Yes | Fully | Nothing new |
| Withholding and cadence substitution | Shore, Tin | Partly | Fully | Composition only |
| Re-dressing one theme (music box, piano, quartet, solo cello) | Civ VI, *Sheepy*, *Last Samurai* | Partly | Yes, with a realism ceiling on strings | Composition only |
| Assembly of parts, stems, last voice by consent | Shore, Prahlow | No | Yes | A layer API in `Music`, synced emitters |
| Diegetic parts on emitters and voice cards | Prahlow, Guthrie and Ipsen | Infrastructure only | Yes | Shared timing; tunes written as parts |
| Time pulse tied to a mechanism | Prahlow, Zimmer, *Sheepy* | Ticks as emitters | Fully | Triggering from game state |
| Reversed orchestra and processed sources | Streitenfeld | No | Fully (array reversal) | Composition only |
| Tape off, full band at the threshold | Tin's saved timbre (by analogy) | Tape on every cue | Fully | A clean render pass |
| Organ-like timbre and sub-bass pedal | Zimmer | No | Approximate (additive) | Care with small speakers |
| Choir and sung text | Shore, Tin | No | Poorly | Singers or a sample library |
| Folk or "home" soloists (banjo, harmonica, whistle, Hardanger) | Prahlow, Shore, Guthrie | No | Poorly | Real players; a whistle the director can record |
| People noises (claps, group voices) | Guthrie and Ipsen | No | Poorly | Real people |
| Low brass blast, taiko and metal percussion | Hemsey, Zimmer, Shore | No | Weakly | Samples, or recorded objects in Streitenfeld's manner |
| Expressive solo cello at the big swells | *Last Samurai*, existing cues | Synthesized | Adequate | A real cellist would be the largest single upgrade |

Three cautions apply to this table. First, a synthetic voice can be the point rather than a compromise: Lober's VOCALOID lead divided listeners and then won them over. That evidence is weak and anecdotal, but it fits a game whose only human voices arrive through a recording chain. Second, Streitenfeld's habit of making every sound himself suggests the cheapest route to percussion. Record the refuge's own objects (a pawl, a ration drum, a tin voice card) rather than license taiko. Third, the theme itself already uses VI–i motion (B♭maj7 to D minor). That is generic harmony, but it sits near the loop that analysts give for *Interstellar*. New cues should develop the lullaby's own falling F–E–D cell rather than drift toward any studied score's progression or figure.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question from "what should Dead Letter's music sound like?" to "what should it hold back, and when should it give it?" Two problems turn out to share an answer. The first is how to make a 160-minute game's swells stay powerful. The second is how to make the ending feel like a delivery rather than a finale. The answer to both is the same form-level economy that Shore and Prahlow practise. Keep the full cadence, the finished birthday tune and the assembled diegetic song unspent until DL-51. Then let the player's voluntary sit add the last voice. The game's existing assets (a music box that is "the parcel's voice", emitters, a recording chain and a lullaby built to be re-dressed) already form the instrument for that plan. Most of it can be done in code.

The weakest link is evidence, not technique. Every composer finding here passed through a search snippet. The highest-value follow-up is to read in full the primary sources the notes list: Adams' two annotated scores, Game Developer's two Prahlow features, the Bandcamp Daily interview with Guthrie and the Static Mass interview with Streitenfeld. Those would confirm the claims this plan leans on hardest. The cheapest test of the plan itself is the playtest plan's existing question ([playtest_plan.md](../docs/playtest_plan.md)), widened: do players who heard the den's music box at DL-09 recognise its part when it returns at the end?

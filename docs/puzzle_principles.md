# Dead Letter: puzzle and pacing principles

**Status:** proposed for director review, 18 October 2026
**Why:** the first playtest of DL-01 to DL-05 took 5:43 against a 15-minute chapter target, with two deliberate failures.
The cinematic flow is working; the gameplay is under-delivered. Each scene is one single-step puzzle on its own
mechanic, and none builds on the last.
**Evidence:** `docs/research/cinematic_puzzle_design.md`, a sourced brief on Limbo, Inside, Little Nightmares, Planet of
Lana, Somerville, Brothers, Unravel, Stray, Cocoon and Sheepy: A Short Adventure. Its tags mark what is sourced and what
is inference. Its web research worked from search excerpts, so check quotes before using them publicly.

These rules sit beside `docs/direction.md`. Where they conflict, raise it; don't pick one silently.

## The rules

### 1. Every mechanic gets an arc: introduce, develop, twist, combine

A mechanic first appears where it is safe and obvious. It then returns with one new complication, then in a role the
player assumed it couldn't have, and finally alongside another mechanic. Then it rests until a later chapter reframes it.

- **Why:** this is Nintendo's "taught, developed, twisted, dropped" structure and Portal's escalation, and how Limbo
  reuses its tools without repeating a puzzle.
- **Our failure:** Chapter 1 introduced five mechanics and developed none of them.
- **Test:** for each mechanic, can you name the room that introduces it, the room that develops it and the room that
  twists it? If not, it isn't finished.

### 2. Each puzzle builds on the one before

Every puzzle room uses at least one thing the player already knows, plus at most one new thing. The last puzzle of a
chapter combines two or three of the chapter's mechanics. The ladder in `docs/chapter1_plan.md` is the contract for
Chapter 1.

- **Watch for:** "same solution, new skin" (Unravel, Brothers). Putting a weight on a thing to move another thing
  four times over is one puzzle, not four. Each step must change what the player has to *think*, not just the props.

### 3. Few elements; the goal is obvious, the method is not

A puzzle room has two to four interactive elements, and Jeppe Carlsen's examples have three.
- **The goal is in frame:** the exit, or what blocks it, is in the first framing of the room.
- **No hunting:** nothing the solution needs is off-screen or hidden, so the player is never searching for "the
  exact spot".
- **Watch for:** the obtuse puzzle (Somerville) and the seven-mechanic puzzle (Carlsen's warning).

### 4. Build the "aha" on a wrong assumption the world corrects

Predict what a first-time player will try, let it half-work or fail visibly, and make the answer an object in a role
they assumed was fixed:
- the trolley that was a weight becomes a step;
- a courier like D-17 becomes the weight;
- the carrier that carried D-17 carries a delivery for him.

A good aha drops a constraint the player never stated. It never comes from hiding something.

### 5. Failure is cheap and teaches

- **Checkpoint after every thought**, not every room, and always just before an execution test.
- **Back in control within three seconds**, with no more than twenty seconds of solved traversal to repeat. This is
  the overview's rule; the research suggests 1.5 seconds where we can.
- **Ambient sound and music keep running through a respawn**, as in Inside.
- **Solved states persist:** a set pawl stays set, and a parked trolley stays parked.
- **Death shows the player something:** where the carrier was going, or what the platform does under weight.
- Sheepy's checkpoints are "ridiculously generous", and players loved it for that.

### 6. Readability is light, colour, motion and linkage

- **Red means usable** (`docs/direction.md`).
- **A light falls on the thing that matters** (the crank's pool).
- **Every lever, crank or plate has a visible cable, rod, chain or pipe** to what it moves, so the player can trace
  cause and effect across the frame without a cut.
- **The play plane stays clean:** nothing in front of or behind the lane looks usable when it isn't.
- **The dark is atmosphere, not an obstacle:** in the dark lower levels, the path and the interactables stay lit.

### 7. Alternate the gears

Don't stay in one mode for more than about three minutes. A chapter runs roughly:

> traverse (30 to 60 s) → puzzle → curiosity or vista → puzzle (develop) → small set piece → puzzle (twist or combine) → emotional beat

As a share of a chapter's first-time play time, aim for about:
- 45 to 55% puzzle, thinking and execution together;
- 25 to 35% traversal, looking and curiosities;
- 10 to 20% set pieces and reveals.

These shares are our estimates; no studio publishes them. Something noticeably new (an object, a hazard, a place or a
story fact) should arrive about every two to three minutes.

### 8. Looking is content, if it shows something new

Quiet beats are legitimate: a vista, a sit, a background event. Carlsen: "maybe the puzzle is to just look at the
landscape." Each must show something new about the world or about D-17, and take under 30 seconds unless it is a
chapter's emotional peak.

### 9. The mechanic is the story

D-17's verbs are a courier's verbs: carry, deliver, sort, push a trolley, present at a terminal. Twists should
carry the theme:
- his own kind as the weight;
- routing a delivery;
- leaving something on charge;
- stopping an empty routine.

The ending should spend a verb, as Sheepy gives up its powers, Brothers takes away a brother and Inside dissolves its
boy.

### 10. Curiosities: playful, meaningful, beside the path

Per scene, one to three small curiosities, each:
- on or beside the critical path, never in a separate hub;
- under 30 seconds;
- never required.

Their reward is meaning or delight, not stats: a story fact, a joke, a sound or a view. Some should simply be
playful, like Sheepy's piano, phone and joke achievement: a parcel scale that weighs D-17, a stamp machine, a tube
that coughs out a letter.

**Visuals are the story language** (director, 18 October 2026). Many players don't read, so every essential story beat
must land through an image, an action or a tableau. Letters, signs and screens add depth, and never carry the only
copy of something the player needs.

Each chapter has one major secret. Ours are **undelivered letters**: D-17 finds one and posts it at a red mail slot,
where it is stamped. Each tells a small story of the refuge, and together they can change the epilogue, as Inside's
orbs do.

### 11. The world tells what happened down there

- **One tableau per scene,** readable in about three seconds (a dried tideline across every home in ring C; a queue
  line painted to a lift marked UP, with dropped belongings).
- **Background events during low-input moments,** without a cut (a cargo lift carrying furniture *up* and never
  coming back).
- **Sparse, chosen props:** one child's shoe beats ten crates.
- **Wordless signs and pictograms** for the history: notices, tallies, timetables, stamps.
- **Core beats unmistakable:** who Mara was, what the parcel is, why the lower levels emptied. Ambiguity stays at the
  edges. Sheepy's one consistent criticism is a vague plot.

### 12. The camera stays with him

One continuous side-on shot; framing changes blend as he moves. Authored shots are reserved for emotional moments,
at most one or two per scene and many scenes none. Gameplay is never shown only in a shot. See "Use the camera" in
`docs/direction.md`.

### 13. Every verb is taught by the space

Crouch, climb, carry, push, pull, ride and post each get a safe first use that the room makes obvious (Sheepy never
teaches crouch, and players noticed). The first use has no failure state.

### 14. Edit hard, and measure

- **Grow by building:** we reach a time target with more distinct rooms and beats, never by slowing a puzzle, adding
  walking or making a solution obscure. Anything that repeats a solved thought is cut (Limbo cut about 70% of its
  material).
- **Measure every scene twice:**
  - its mechanical floor, with the simulated player and no hesitation;
  - first-time play, with real playtesters timed, plus the simulated player as a rough guide.
- **Chapter 1's acceptance bar:**
  - a floor of at least 8 minutes (it is 3:54 now);
  - a first-time median of 15 to 20 minutes.

## What this changes in practice

- **Chapter 1 is re-planned in `docs/chapter1_plan.md`:**
  - nine scenes, not five;
  - each mechanic runs its arc;
  - a set-piece finale that combines the chapter's mechanics;
  - a story spine for what happened in the lower levels;
  - curiosities and the first undelivered letters.
- **Scene cards** gain a line for each rule: the mechanic's arc step, what it builds on, the wrong assumption, the
  checkpoint after each thought, the linkage, the gear, the curiosity, the tableau and the emotional shot (if any).
